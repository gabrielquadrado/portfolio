Summary

RaiseRight is a fundraising platform where families earn money for schools and organizations by purchasing gift cards from 750+ brands. First-order conversion had been declining, and the existing onboarding was feature-led and educational — it explained the product but didn't motivate behavior change. I designed and led research on a new CBT-informed onboarding flow that treated the core challenge not as "how do I use this app" but as "am I willing to change how I pay for everyday purchases." The new 9-step guided experience uses goal-setting, cognitive restructuring, and self-efficacy building to turn sign-ups into committed users. Moderated research with 7 participants validated the framework — 5 of 7 showed positive confidence shifts — while surfacing critical design fixes that directly shaped the next iteration.

Background

RaiseRight helps families fundraise by earning a percentage back on gift card purchases from brands they already shop at — groceries, gas, dining, travel, and more. Unlike traditional fundraising that asks families to sell products to others, RaiseRight works by shifting existing spending habits. The challenge is that this model requires users to understand a non-obvious mechanic (buy a gift card, use it like normal, percentage goes to your organization) and then commit to changing a core daily behavior: how they pay.

The Problem

First-order conversion was declining. The existing post-registration onboarding was feature-led — it explained what RaiseRight does but didn't address why users should change their behavior or whether they believed they could. Users were signing up but not transacting. The onboarding treated conversion like a typical e-commerce funnel, but RaiseRight isn't a typical purchase — it's asking people to learn, understand, and decide to change how they pay, a core habit in their life.

The Hypothesis

We hypothesized that adding intentional friction — goal-setting, self-reflection, and personalized projections — would increase the quality of users who complete onboarding and drive higher first-order conversion and retention. The bet was that spending more time on a guided, on-the-rails experience rooted in behavioral science would outperform a shorter, information-only flow. Leadership aligned on tolerating a 35–40% drop-off during onboarding if it produced meaningfully higher post-onboarding activation.

The Solution

A 9-step guided onboarding flow grounded in Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) principles. Each step maps to a specific behavioral mechanism: value anchoring, goal specificity, cognitive restructuring (identifying and overcoming barriers), self-efficacy building (personalized earning projections), and behavioral activation (selecting brands and taking a first action). The flow was designed mobile-first, prototyped as a navigable Figma prototype with variables, and validated through moderated user research before A/B testing.

DISCOVER & FRAME

Before designing any screens, I needed to understand why this onboarding was fundamentally different from a typical product onboarding. RaiseRight doesn't just need users to learn a feature — it needs them to commit to changing how they pay for everyday purchases. That's a behavioral shift, not an information gap. I started by studying apps that face the same challenge: products where success depends on sustained habit change, not just comprehension.

Competitor Analysis: How Habit-Change Apps Onboard

I analyzed onboarding flows from apps that require behavioral commitment — Calm, Noom, Headspace, Fitbit, and others. These apps don't just explain features; they use structured psychological techniques to build motivation before asking for action. The patterns I identified mapped directly to CBT principles that are well-established in behavioral psychology.

Apps like Calm and Noom front-load emotional investment by asking users to articulate why they're here before showing what the app does. Noom asks about your relationship with food before showing any diet features. Calm asks what's on your mind before offering meditation categories. This isn't just personalization — it's a CBT technique called value anchoring that increases persistence under difficulty.

Multi-select preference screens serve a dual purpose: they collect data for personalization, but they also function as micro-commitments — each tap is a small behavioral investment that increases the psychological cost of abandoning the flow.

Earning projections and progress visualization map to the CBT concept of self-efficacy building — showing users evidence that their goal is achievable based on their own data, not abstract promises.

Mapping CBT to Onboarding Design

CBT provides a structured framework for behavior change that translates directly into UX design decisions. I mapped the core CBT principles to onboarding steps, creating a design rationale that tied every screen to a specific behavioral mechanism. This wasn't decoration — it was the architecture of the flow.

Phase 1 — Anchoring & Definition

CBT: Goal Specificity + Value Alignment. Before asking users to do anything, connect the habit to their identity and values. "What are you fundraising for?" and "How much do you want to earn?" These aren't data collection — they're commitment devices. CBT research shows that specific, personally meaningful goals increase persistence more than vague intentions.

Phase 2 — Cognitive Restructuring

CBT: Identifying Barriers + Psychoeducation. Surface the automatic negative thoughts that block action. "I'm nervous about linking my bank account." Instead of ignoring these fears, we externalize them. Naming the barrier is the first step to dismantling it. Then we provide targeted evidence that directly addresses each concern.

Phase 3 — Reassurance & Evidence

CBT: Building Self-Efficacy + Rationale. Replace fear with facts. A personalized earning projection — tied to the user's own spending categories and goal — shows that the behavior change is both achievable and worthwhile. The calculator visualizes the gap between doing nothing and using RaiseRight, framing the habit not as effort but as capturing lost value.

Phase 4 — Behavioral Activation

CBT: Implementation Intentions + Minimum Viable Habit. We're not asking users to "shop." We're asking them to decide: "When I go to Safeway, I will use RaiseRight." Selecting specific brands creates an implementation intention — a concrete if-then plan that dramatically increases follow-through.

Stakeholder Alignment & Constraints

I worked with the Head of Product and PM to align on success metrics and risk tolerance before design began. Key decisions from our alignment:

Acceptable drop-off

Leadership was willing to tolerate 35–40% onboarding drop-off if it produced higher-quality users who actually transact. This freed me to design a longer, more intentional flow without optimizing for completion rate alone.

Primary metrics

First-order conversion rate (primary), second purchase rate (secondary). We'd measure these through an A/B test against the existing onboarding.

Micro-action hypothesis

We identified specific in-flow actions we believed were the strongest indicators of habit adoption: setting a specific dollar goal, selecting spending categories, choosing 3+ brands, and enabling notifications.

Mobile-first

The flow had to work primarily on mobile, where the majority of RaiseRight signups originate.

DESIGN

With the CBT framework and stakeholder alignment in place, I moved into design. The process went from UX flow mapping to wireframes to a fully interactive hi-fi prototype built with Figma Variables for the user research sessions.

UX Flow: The 9-Step Guided Onboarding

I collaborated with the Head of Product to map out the full UX flow before any visual design. Each step was tied to a specific CBT principle and had a clear behavioral objective. The flow progresses through three emotional arcs: curiosity (Steps 1–2), commitment (Steps 3–5), and activation (Steps 6–9).

Step 1 — Value Prop

CBT: Social Proof + Trust. Lead with credibility: $1,000+ earned by top families, 30 years of operation, 1,000+ brands. The goal isn't to explain the product — it's to establish that this is real, proven, and worth the user's time.

Step 2 — What For

CBT: Value Anchoring. "What are you fundraising for?" This connects the abstract act of buying gift cards to a concrete, emotionally resonant purpose — their child's tuition, sports, music.

Step 3 — Goal Setting

CBT: Goal Specificity. A dollar amount and timeline. The monthly breakdown ($37.50/mo instead of $450/year) makes the goal feel manageable. The micro-copy "People who set a goal are more likely to earn" uses social proof to boost commitment.

Step 4 — Current Habits

CBT: Baseline Assessment. "Which of these do you do already?" — Groceries, gas, dining, shopping, travel. This primes users to see RaiseRight as fitting into their existing life rather than adding effort.

Step 5 — Projected Earnings

CBT: Self-Efficacy Building. A personalized calculator tied to their selected categories and goal. Users toggle categories on/off and watch the projection change in real-time. Tier badges (Explorer → Supporter → Champion) add a gamification layer.

Step 6 — How to Earn

CBT: Psychoeducation. The mechanism explanation: buy a gift card through RaiseRight, use it like normal, a percentage goes to your goal. Deliberately placed after goal-setting — users are more receptive to "how" once they've committed to "how much."

Step 7 — Favorites

CBT: Implementation Intentions. Select brands you already shop at. This creates the "I already do this" moment — the single strongest self-efficacy signal in the flow.

Steps 8–9 — Notifications & Take Action

CBT: Reinforcement Setup + Behavioral Activation. Notifications framed as a goal accelerator, not a generic permission request. Step 9 closes the loop: you set a goal, you picked brands, now buy your first gift card.

Wireframes

I started with concept wireframes to validate the flow structure and screen sequencing with the team before investing in visual design. These wireframes focused on information hierarchy, interaction patterns (sliders, toggles, multi-select), and the emotional pacing of the experience.

Key design decisions at this stage included the goal slider with monthly breakdown, the category toggle system for the earnings calculator, and the progressive disclosure of the earning mechanism.

Hi-Fidelity Prototype with Figma Variables

The final prototype was built in Figma using Variables to create a fully navigable, state-aware experience. Users could set a real dollar goal, toggle spending categories and see the earning projection update dynamically, select brands, and move through the entire 9-step flow as if using the real app. This level of fidelity was critical for the think-aloud research protocol — we needed participants to react to the experience, not puzzle over placeholder content. The prototype was designed mobile-first and shared via screen-share during 60-minute moderated research sessions.

RESEARCH

With the prototype ready, I planned and led a moderated research study to validate the CBT-informed onboarding before committing to an A/B test. This wasn't a usability test — it was a behavioral validation study. I needed to understand not just whether users could complete the flow, but whether it changed how they felt about committing to the behavior change.

Research Plan

I designed a 45–60 minute moderated interview protocol with three parts: a warm-up to understand the participant's fundraising context, a guided walkthrough of the prototype using a think-aloud protocol, and a reflection debrief.

The critical measurement was a pre/post confidence delta. Before seeing the prototype, I asked each participant: "On a scale of 1 to 5, how likely are you to change how you pay for everyday purchases to support a fundraising goal in a cashback app?" After completing the flow, I asked the same question but specifically referencing RaiseRight. A positive delta — post score higher than pre — was the primary success signal.

Participants

I recruited 7 participants through UserInterviews.com (1 no-show from 8 recruited). The cohort was intentionally diverse across age, income, geography, family size, and device type. All were parents actively involved in school fundraising who buy gift cards at least monthly.

Key profiles included a clinical psychologist (uniquely qualified to evaluate the CBT framing), a "points hacker" who'd previously used RaiseRight (stress test for the value proposition), a family of six on a strict budget (where earning projections carry real financial weight), and a father familiar with scrip programs (fastest comprehension baseline).

Key Findings

Emotional Journey Map

The emotional journey across the 9 steps followed a clear pattern: strong initial curiosity (Step 1, score 4.0), a dip through the commitment-heavy middle steps where projection credibility and mechanic confusion created friction (Steps 5–6, score 3.0), a peak at notifications where 7/7 participants agreed without hesitation (Step 8, score 4.5), and a mixed landing at the Take Action screen where copy contradictions undermined the activation moment (Step 9, score 3.2).

Pre/Post Confidence Delta

5 of 7 participants showed a positive or maintained-high confidence delta — the primary success signal for the study. The average post-score was 4.5/5. Two participants showed negative deltas, but for distinct and instructive reasons: one entered at 5/5 and dropped due to unresolved confusion about the gift card mechanic (design-recoverable), and the other was a prior RaiseRight user whose ROI calculus against credit card travel points was a product-market fit issue for her specific profile, not an onboarding design failure.

Critical Insights

1. The Gift Card Mental Model Never Resolves

No participant read the gift card mechanic as obvious — all guessed. 5 of 7 were still confused at the end of the flow. One participant said she'd leave the app to verify it on Google before committing. Highest-severity drop-off risk in the entire study.

2. Earning Projections Trigger Skepticism Before Self-Efficacy

4 of 7 described projected earnings over $1,000 as "unbelievable" or "suspicious" until they saw specific brand percentages. Users saw the "how much" before understanding the "how," which inverted the intended confidence-building sequence.

3. The Deadline Gap in Goal Setting

6 of 7 participants fundraise on event-driven windows of 6 weeks to 6 months — not annual cycles. The fixed one-year timeframe felt abstract and disconnected from their actual urgency, reducing the accountability the goal was designed to create.

4. Brand Selection Cap Creates Frustration

5 of 7 wanted to select more than 3 brands. One participant concluded she was permanently earning-locked to only those 3 brands. The copy "Choose 3 favorite brands" was ambiguous enough to produce three different misreadings.

5. "How to Earn" Belongs Earlier

5 of 7 wanted to understand the earning mechanism before being asked to set a goal or evaluate projections. In the final interview, when I moved the "How to Earn" screen to Step 2, the participant showed significantly higher comprehension and confidence across the entire remaining flow.

6. The $5 Reward Copy Contradicts Itself

"Reward Unlocked" implies the user has something; "Place 3 orders to earn" implies they don't. This ambiguity at the activation moment caused 5 of 7 to choose "Explore on my own" instead of tapping a brand card — the intended first action failed to convert.

Friction Heatmap

The top friction type was copy and language, affecting 6 of 9 steps. Most fixes were low-effort wording changes, not redesigns. The second highest was feature expectation, affecting 7 of 9 steps. Trust and credibility friction was concentrated entirely in Step 5 — fix the mechanism explanation and it likely resolves.

ITERATE

Research findings translated directly into a prioritized set of design iterations, categorized by severity: P1 (critical — blocking activation), P2 (high — degrading experience quality). Each recommendation tied back to specific participant data and behavioral signals.

Recommended Design Changes

P1 — Clarify the gift card mechanic (Steps 6 + 9)

Replace abstract step labels with a single concrete sentence: "Buy a gift card through RaiseRight, use it like normal, and a percentage goes straight to your goal." Add a persistent info icon on brand cards in Step 9. Evidence: 7/7 guessed the mechanic; no one read it as obvious.

P1 — Replace fixed timeframe with a due date picker (Step 3)

Allow users to set a specific fundraising deadline instead of defaulting to one year. Monthly breakdown updates dynamically. Evidence: 6/7 fundraise on short windows (6 weeks to 6 months).

P1 — Fix the $5 reward copy (Step 9)

Separate the unlock state from the earn condition. Replace "Reward Unlocked" with forward-looking framing: "Place 3 orders in 30 days and earn a $5 bonus for your goal." Explicitly state the $5 goes to the fundraising goal. Evidence: 3/7 confused by contradiction; 4/7 didn't know where $5 goes.

P1 — Move "How to Earn" to Step 2 (Flow restructuring)

Tested in final interview — participant showed significantly higher comprehension across the entire remaining flow. Embed a mechanism reminder inside the Step 5 accordion. Evidence: 5/7 wanted the mechanic earlier; 3/7 found projections unbelievable without it.

P2 — Reframe brand selection as "at least 3" (Step 7)

Update copy and interaction so users can select more. Surface top earners from the full selection on Step 9. Evidence: 5/7 wanted more; 1/7 believed she was earning-locked permanently.

P2 — Add type-in field for goal amount (Step 3)

Make the number directly editable by tapping. Slider remains as visual aid. Evidence: 3/7 requested type-in; one user would enter $17K tuition — slider fails completely at that scale.

P2 — Add per-brand earning rates to projection (Step 5)

Show specific percentages (e.g., "Starbucks: 9%, Walmart: 4%") inside the accordion. Let skeptical users verify the math themselves. Evidence: Multiple participants ran mental audits; per-brand data resolved skepticism when available.

CONCLUSION

The core hypothesis — that added friction via goal-setting and psychoeducation increases commitment — was largely supported. 6 of 7 users reported positive or maintained-high interest in the app (4.5/5 average post-score) specifically because they saw how shifting their existing spending toward brands they already use could meet their fundraising goals. The CBT framework successfully moved users from abstract curiosity to concrete behavioral intention.

The research also revealed that the sequence of information matters as much as the information itself. Showing earning projections before explaining the earning mechanism inverted the confidence-building arc. Moving "How to Earn" to Step 2 — tested in the final interview — resolved this and produced the highest comprehension score of any session.

Key Results

  • 5 of 7 participants showed a positive pre/post confidence delta — the primary success metric.
  • 7 of 7 would enable notifications when framed as a goal accelerator — the strongest unanimity in the study.
  • 6 of 7 said the onboarding length felt "about right" or "manageable" — validating the intentional friction hypothesis.
  • 4.5/5 average post-onboarding confidence score — up from 3.9/5 pre-score.

Key Learnings

  • Behavioral onboarding works, but sequence is everything. The CBT framework was validated — users who understood the "why" before the "how much" showed higher confidence and lower friction. The single most impactful iteration was a flow resequence, not a visual redesign.
  • Trust is built with specifics, not totals. Aggregate earning projections triggered skepticism. Per-brand percentages triggered mental math that resolved into belief. Users trust numbers they can verify against their own experience.
  • Name the fear to dismantle it. The barriers screen (cognitive restructuring) was one of the most effective steps. Users felt validated rather than judged, and the targeted rebuttals directly addressed their automatic negative thoughts. This is CBT working exactly as intended in a product context.
  • Copy is the highest-leverage design material. The top friction type across the entire study was copy and language — affecting 6 of 9 steps. Most fixes were wording changes, not interaction redesigns. In behavioral onboarding, every word is a design decision.
