Summary

A CBT-informed onboarding redesign for RaiseRight, a gift card fundraising app where parents earn money for schools and organizations by shopping at partner brands. The existing feature-led onboarding wasn't moving the behavior change the product depended on, and first-order conversion was declining. I designed a 9-step mobile-first flow grounded in behavioral science principles, validated it across two moderated research rounds, and shipped a redesign that produced zero confidence drops in Round 2 across all participants. The single load-bearing insight: solving how participants understand the gift card mechanic was a prerequisite to every other improvement in the flow.

Background

RaiseRight is a fundraising app for parents raising money for schools, sports teams, and youth organizations. The core mechanic is unusual: participants buy gift cards through the app for their own everyday use (groceries, gas, restaurants), and a percentage of each purchase flows directly to their organization's fundraising goal. It turns regular spending into fundraising at no extra cost to the buyer, but it depends on participants understanding that they are the ones using the gift cards, not gifting them.

The Problem

First-order conversion was trending down. The existing onboarding was feature-led: it explained what the app does and listed earning categories without engaging with the behavioral shift the product actually requires. Participants were arriving at the first purchase moment without enough belief, motivation, or understanding to act. The team needed an onboarding that did more than inform. It needed to persuade.

The Hypothesis

Intentional friction grounded in behavioral science would produce higher-quality activations than a faster, lower-friction flow. Users who understood the mechanic, set a real goal, and pictured their own brands in the flow would be more likely to make a first purchase, even if some dropped off along the way. Joe accepted a 35 to 40% drop-off tolerance as the trade for higher conversion among the users who finished. This budget unlocked the design space for a deliberate, CBT-informed flow.

The Approach

A 9-step CBT-informed onboarding flow, mobile-first, built as a clickable Figma prototype using Figma Variables for persistent selections across screens. Two rounds of moderated research validated the approach: Round 1 surfaced the core comprehension problem, Round 2 validated the iterations with zero confidence drops across all participants.

DISCOVER & FRAME

The first decision was a framing one. The team had been treating onboarding as an information gap: users needed to learn what the app does. The research evidence pointed in a different direction. This wasn't a knowledge problem. It was a behavioral shift problem. Users don't fail to use RaiseRight because they don't know what it is. They fail to use it because changing how you pay for groceries is genuinely hard, even when the upside is clear. Reframing onboarding as a guided behavioral shift, not a feature walkthrough, set the direction for everything that followed.

Competitor Analysis: How Habit-Change Apps Onboard

I studied three apps that built their onboarding around behavioral shift rather than feature explanation: Calm, Noom, and Headspace. Each is in a different category, but each solved the same underlying problem RaiseRight faces. They moved users from "this sounds nice" to "I'm going to actually do this," and they did it by leveraging behavioral science principles rather than feature lists.

Value anchoring appeared in all three. Each app showed users a personalized projection of what they could achieve before asking them to commit to anything. Calm shows hours of sleep gained. Noom shows pounds lost. Headspace shows minutes of calmer days ahead. The number wasn't the feature, it was the motivational anchor that made the rest of the flow feel worth the friction.

Micro-commitments showed up as a sequence pattern. None of these apps ask the user a single big question at the start. They ask a series of small ones: your name, your goal, your current habits, your reasons. By the time the user reaches the value proposition, they've already invested in the flow.

Self-efficacy framing was the most subtle and the most important. These apps don't just tell users they can succeed. They show them, through projected outcomes and familiar inputs, that success is in reach for someone like them. This is what RaiseRight's existing onboarding lacked entirely.

Mapping CBT to Onboarding Design

I structured the flow around four CBT-informed phases. Each phase has a different cognitive job, and each step within a phase contributes to that phase's goal. This isn't a literal therapy framework, it's CBT applied as a design lens: identify the limiting belief, restructure it, build evidence for the new belief, and translate belief into behavior.

Phase 1 — Anchoring & Definition (Steps 1 to 2)

Establish what the app is and how it works before asking for anything. The value prop introduces social proof and platform credibility. The How To Earn screen, moved to Step 2 after Round 1 research, gives users the mechanism explanation upfront so every later screen has context.

Phase 2 — Cognitive Restructuring (Steps 3 to 4)

Reframe the user's relationship to their own spending. Goal setting attaches a specific dollar amount and deadline to a real fundraising event. Habits gives users a sense that their existing spending is already most of the way there. The cognitive shift is from "I'd have to do something new" to "I'd have to do something I'm already doing, slightly differently."

Phase 3 — Reassurance & Evidence (Steps 5 to 7)

Build belief that the goal is achievable. The earning projection turns the user's selected categories into a personalized number. Favorites lets users see their own brands and confirm "I already do this." Notifications convert intention into commitment by enabling the bonus alerts that drive return visits.

Phase 4 — Behavioral Activation (Steps 8 to 9)

Convert commitment into action. The transition celebration validates what the user has just done. The Take Action screen presents the user's selected brands as the primary action, removing every barrier between the new belief and the first transaction.

Stakeholder Alignment & Constraints

Acceptable drop-off

Joe agreed that 35 to 40% drop-off through the flow was acceptable if the users who finished converted at meaningfully higher rates. This budget made the difference: it allowed for a 9-step flow when stakeholder instinct might have pushed for 3 or 4.

Primary metrics

First-order conversion was the top metric. Secondary measures included pre/post confidence delta from moderated research, used as the strategic success signal across both rounds.

Micro-action hypothesis

We agreed that micro-actions during onboarding, like setting a goal, selecting habits, choosing favorite brands, would correlate with first-purchase activation. Each micro-action was both a research probe and a real product moment.

Mobile-first constraint

The full flow was scoped to mobile from day one. Web onboarding would follow later, building on the design language and behavioral framing validated here.

DESIGN

The design work split into three threads running in parallel: shaping the 9-step flow as a coherent sequence, designing the screens to a level where they could carry real research, and iterating on the highest-risk screen (How To Earn) through a deliberate variant exploration. The flow needed to feel inevitable to the participant, which meant every step had to earn its place in the sequence.

9-Step UX Flow

Step 1 — Value Prop (Anchoring)

Personalized greeting and three credibility stats: families earn $1,000+ per year, 30 years helping 50K+ organizations, 1,000+ brands. Sets the platform as credible before any ask.

Step 2 — How To Earn (Anchoring)

The mechanism explanation, moved here from its original Step 6 position after Round 1. A concrete brand example (Gap at 14%) lets users see real earnings before any projection screen.

Step 3 — Goal Setting (Cognitive Restructuring)

Goal amount with a due date picker (added after Round 1). Per-period breakdown logic adapts to the timeline: gaps under 56 days display in weeks, 56+ days in whole months.

Step 4 — Habits (Cognitive Restructuring)

Users select spending categories they already use. Reframes onboarding from "learn something new" to "tell us what you already do."

Step 5 — Earning Projection (Reassurance)

A personalized number based on the user's selected categories, with category toggles that update the projection live. Tier movement (Explorer → Champion) adds motivational pull.

Step 6 — Habits Confirmation (Reassurance)

Quick reinforcement of the categories selected. (Originally also contained How To Earn before Round 1's restructure.)

Step 7 — Favorites (Reassurance)

Users select brands they already shop at. The "3 or more brands" copy (reframed after Round 1) avoids the earning-cap misread.

Step 8 — Notifications (Activation)

Opt-in to bonus earning alerts. The strongest, most consistent step in the entire flow across both rounds.

Step 9 — Take Action (Activation)

Selected brands appear as the primary action, the user's first real transaction moment. The $5 reward (later removed) was originally positioned as a P.S. below the brand tiles.

Wireframes

The concept wireframes prioritized sequence and information density above all else. The driving question at this stage was not what each screen looks like, but whether the flow as a whole carries a participant from skepticism to action without losing momentum. I worked through layout decisions on cheap fidelity first so the higher-stakes screens (Goal Setting, Earning Projection, Take Action) could be tested in hi-fi without rebuilding the structure.

Hi-Fidelity Prototype

The hi-fi prototype was built in Figma using Variables so participant selections persisted across screens. When a participant chose a goal amount in Step 3, that amount appeared on the projection screen in Step 5 and on the Take Action screen in Step 9. When they selected favorite brands in Step 7, those exact brands appeared as the recommended starting points in Step 9. This persistence was essential for moderated research: it let participants experience the flow as if it were already a real product, which is what made the pre/post confidence delta measurement meaningful.

TEST — ROUND 1

Round 1 was a behavioral validation study, not a usability test. The goal wasn't to find tap targets and broken flows. It was to find out whether a CBT-informed onboarding actually moves user belief. I ran 7 moderated sessions over three days in March, walking each participant through the clickable prototype using a think-aloud protocol and a pre/post confidence probe.

Research Plan

Methodology

Pre/post confidence delta. Before the walkthrough, each participant rated themselves on a scale of 1 to 5: "How likely are you to change how you pay for everyday purchases to support a fundraising goal in a cashback app?" After the walkthrough, the same question, with "specifically with RaiseRight" appended. The delta is the primary signal. Did the flow move them?

Participants

7 active fundraising parents recruited via userinterviews.com. Mixed income brackets, mixed gift card familiarity, mixed prior cashback app experience.

Notable profiles

  • M.P. had prior scrip program experience from his kids' schools, which became a critical proof-of-concept reference point
  • J.B. was a self-described "points hacker" who had previously used RaiseRight, making her a useful skeptic test

Key Findings

Emotional Journey Map

The journey traced from cautious curiosity at the value prop (4.0 average sentiment) to a sharp drop at the projection screen (3.0) and How To Earn screen (2.5), then partial recovery at Favorites (3.5) and a final mixed-to-confused state at Take Action (3.2). The shape of the curve told the story: trust was built early, broken in the middle, partially recovered, and left unresolved at the end. The two lowest points were both about the same underlying problem.

Pre/Post Confidence Delta

Five of seven participants finished with a positive delta. Two dropped. The drops are the interesting data:

  • M.M., 5 → 3 (−2): The largest drop in the study and the most design-actionable. She entered at maximum confidence, called the flow "effortless," and then lost two full points because the projection felt unbelievable without a mechanism explanation and the gift card mechanic stayed unresolved at the end. She named external research about gift cards as her next step before using the app.
  • J.B., 3 → 2 (−1): Important outlier. J.B. is a points hacker who previously used RaiseRight, ran the math on the projection screen, and concluded writing a check was a better ROI than spending $5K to $7K to earn $500 back. Her drop reflects product-market fit for her profile, not a fixable UX issue.

The split between M.M.'s design-recoverable drop and J.B.'s product-fit drop is the lens that shaped what to iterate on. M.M.'s issues were exactly what I would fix. J.B.'s were not.

Critical Insights

01

Gift card mental model never resolves

Seven of seven participants guessed the mechanic correctly, but no one read it as obvious. Five of seven were still confused at the end of the flow. This was the load-bearing problem: every downstream issue depended on it.

02

Projections trigger skepticism before self-efficacy

Three of seven called the earning numbers "almost unbelievable" without understanding the mechanism first. The "See how it's calculated" accordion made things worse for two of them, because it explained the math without explaining where the money comes from.

03

Deadline gap in goal setting

Six of seven fundraise on event-based windows of 6 weeks to 6 months, not annual cycles. The fixed one-year timeframe felt abstract and detached from any real deadline. Three participants explicitly asked for a due date input instead of a duration.

04

Brand selection cap creates frustration

Five of seven were frustrated by the 3-brand cap. Four of seven drew the more serious conclusion that the cap also limits where they can earn, not just what's shown. The copy "Choose 3 favorite brands" was ambiguous enough to produce that misread.

05

"How to Earn" belongs earlier

Six of seven felt the placement at Step 6 was too late. The mechanic explanation needed to come before the projection screen, not after.

06

$5 reward copy contradicts itself

Three of seven were confused by "$5 reward unlocked" conflicting with "place 3 orders to earn." Four of seven didn't know whether the $5 goes to them or to the fundraising goal. Five of seven chose "Explore on my own" over the recommended brand cards.

Friction Heatmap

The heatmap concentrated friction in two places: Step 5 (Projection) and Step 6 (How To Earn). Almost every problem in the flow traced back to copy and language (highest friction type) or feature expectation (second highest). Trust and credibility friction was isolated entirely to Step 5, which meant fixing the mechanism explanation upstream would likely resolve it.

ITERATE

With the findings synthesized, I built a P1/P2 priority framework grounded in the severity of each flag and the leverage of each change. P1 items were changes that, if shipped, would unblock multiple downstream issues. P2 items were high-value but not gating.

Iterations Overview

Recommended Design Changes

P1

Clarify the gift card mechanic

Replace the abstract three-step layout on the How To Earn screen with a single concrete sentence: "Buy a gift card through RaiseRight, use it like normal, and a percentage goes straight to your goal." Add a persistent info icon on the brand cards in Step 9. Directly addresses Flag 01, the highest-severity drop-off risk in the study.

P1

Move "How to Earn" to Step 2

Resequence the flow so the mechanism explanation comes before the projection screen, not after. Four of seven Round 1 participants explicitly wanted this. The relocation makes every downstream screen carry context it previously lacked.

P1

Replace fixed timeframe with due date picker

Swap the one-year fixed timeframe for a due date picker, consistent with the existing web product. The monthly breakdown updates dynamically based on the selected date. Default to one year for users without a specific event in mind.

P1

Fix the $5 reward copy

Replace "Reward unlocked" with forward-looking framing that sets a clear condition: "Place 3 orders in 30 days and earn a $5 bonus." Explicitly state that the $5 goes to the user's wallet, not the fundraising goal. (Later removed entirely after Round 2.)

P2

Reframe brand selection as "at least 3"

Change the copy from "Choose 3 favorite brands" to "Select 3 or more." Update the button behavior so users can select more if they want. Resolves both the selection frustration and the earning-limit misread.

P2

Add a type-in field for goal amount

Make the goal amount directly editable. The slider works for most users but breaks down for large amounts; E.I. set a $17K tuition goal that's effectively unusable with the current input.

P2

Add per-brand earning rates to the projection

Inside the "See how it's calculated" accordion, show per-brand rates rather than aggregate category averages (e.g., Starbucks 9%, Walmart 4%). Three of seven Round 1 participants wanted to verify the math at the granular level.

Variant Exploration — How To Earn

Round 1 made the case for moving How To Earn to Step 2 and rewriting it from scratch. The team had two competing instincts on what the new screen should do. Rather than guess which one was right, I turned the disagreement into a research question and built two variants to test side by side.

1 Tested · Round 2 Group A

A single concrete walkthrough of the gift card mechanic with no other earning modes shown. Depth on the most complex case, no breadth.

2 Tested · Round 2 Group B Shipped

A tabbed layout with full examples for each of the four earning modes: gift cards, online shopping, dining, and travel. Users could navigate between tabs and see the mechanic land for each mode. Equal weight to all four ways to earn.

Both variants went into Round 2: Group A tested Variant 1, Group B tested Variant 2. The split gave a direct read on whether users needed exposure to all four earning modes during onboarding or whether the gift card example alone was sufficient.

Cross-Functional Decisions

The hardest iteration work wasn't the design changes

It was the cross-functional decisions that surrounded them. Two moments stand out.

Joe and Michelle had opposing instincts on the How To Earn screen. Joe pushed for depth on the gift card mechanic, including a sharp before/after framing that referenced credit and debit card earnings percentages. Michelle pushed back on the credit card framing because it would invite unfavorable comparisons ("but my card gives 5%") and undermine the four-mode earning ecosystem the brand positions equally. Rather than guess which approach was right, I built two variants reflecting each underlying instinct: Variant 1 leaning into Joe's depth-on-gift-cards view and Variant 2 leaning into Michelle's four-modes-equal view, and tested them side by side in Round 2.

Late-stage marketing copy suggestions arrived after research had already validated the existing copy. Michelle proposed warmer, more conversational tone changes ("Awesome, let's help you reach that goal!") for screens that had tested well. I pushed back, drawing a distinction that mattered: marketing copy and product copy serve different cognitive contexts. Marketing copy is for browsing users with attention to spare. Product copy in an onboarding flow is for users in the middle of a task where every extra word adds load. I proposed treating the tonal changes as an A/B test or a future research round rather than swapping validated copy on faith. Then I followed up with a warmer note acknowledging the directional goal of conversational tone, because the relationship is what makes the next round of work possible.

VALIDATE — ROUND 2

Round 2 wasn't about finding new problems. It was about validating that the iterations actually moved the needle on the issues Round 1 surfaced. I ran 6 moderated sessions over four days in April, splitting participants into two groups to test a specific open question alongside the broader validation: does the gift card example alone build enough understanding, or do users need exposure to all four earning modes?

What Round 2 Confirmed

Projection skepticism resolved

With the mechanism explanation moved to Step 2, users hit the projection screen with the Gap example already in mind. The "almost unbelievable" reaction from Round 1 didn't reappear. B.H. went from 3 to 5 specifically because the projection screen became persuasive.

Gift card mechanic landed for most

The concrete brand example up front did the comprehension work. L.K. described the new mental model accurately and unprompted. Some residual confusion remained at the Take Action screen, narrower than Round 1 and more recoverable.

Date picker resonated

Users engaged with the date picker rather than leaving it at the default, and the per-period breakdown adapted accordingly.

Energy at the end of the flow

Users finished the flow with momentum, not fatigue. A.K. went in expecting tedium and came out the other side surprised. That tonal shift was the strategic friction hypothesis paying off in real participant words.

Decision Moment

Removing the $5 reward

The $5 reward block did not fully resolve in Round 2. Even with the revised hierarchy (reward repositioned as a P.S. below the brand tiles) and the rewritten copy, participants were still uncertain about what the reward was and where it would go. Rather than continue iterating on copy that was working against the screen's main goal (drive the first transaction), we made the call to remove the reward element entirely. Cutting a feature is a harder design decision than polishing one, and it was the right one. The Take Action screen could now do its actual job without competing claims for attention.

Variant Comparison Outcome

Both variants resolved the gift card mechanic comprehension gap Round 1 had surfaced. Variant 2, the tabs version, was selected as the shipped direction. Users navigated between the four tabs naturally and built a fuller mental model of how the product works as a whole, not just the gift card slice. Variant 1's depth made the mechanic land for Group A, but Group B's exposure to all four modes produced a more complete picture of what the product actually does. Shipping the tabs version aligned with the brand's four-mode positioning while still letting users dig into the detail for each one.

CONCLUSION

The shape of this project is the arc of a comprehension problem. Round 1 found that no participant understood the gift card mechanic without guessing, and that this one issue cascaded into every other friction point in the flow. The earning projection felt unbelievable because users didn't understand where the money came from. The Take Action screen converted poorly because users weren't sure what they were activating. The fixed timeframe felt abstract because the goal had no concrete purchase pattern attached. Solving the mechanic was the prerequisite to solving everything downstream. That single insight reshaped the sequence, the copy, and the variant exploration that followed.

Round 2 validated the iterations with zero confidence drops across all participants and two full-point gains from the most skeptical entrants. The reward element was removed rather than further iterated on, because the screen worked better without it. The flow was handed off to engineering with the structural decisions validated and the next signals defined: icon tap rates as a fake door test for whether to invest in per-vertical content, and an open question on whether tonal copy changes would survive an A/B test against the research-validated baseline.

Key Results

  • Round 2: zero confidence drops across all 6 participants, including two full-point gains from the most skeptical entrants.
  • Round 1: 5 of 7 positive confidence delta, with both drops driven by issues that Round 2 specifically resolved.
  • 7 of 7 participants in Round 1 enabled notifications, the strongest and most consistent step in the entire flow across both rounds.
  • 6 of 7 participants in Round 1 said the onboarding length felt right (or wanted it longer), validating the strategic friction hypothesis.
  • $5 reward element removed from the Take Action screen based on validated Round 2 evidence that it was working against the flow's main goal.

What's Next

Engineering handoff was approaching when research wrapped. The next monitoring plan tracks icon tap rates on the How To Earn screen as a fake door signal, with a suggested 30% threshold to justify investing in per-vertical content for online shopping, dining, and travel earning modes. Marketing's proposed tonal changes remain an open question, scoped as a future A/B test or research round rather than a swap on validated copy. The coordinator experience, designed by Claire for web, is the natural next surface for the same behavioral framing.

Key Learnings

  • Sequence is the highest-leverage lever in onboarding. Moving the How To Earn screen from Step 6 to Step 2 did more for comprehension than any copy rewrite. The right information at the wrong moment doesn't land.
  • Trust comes from specifics, not polish. A real brand at a real percentage ("Gap at 14%") moved the projection screen from unbelievable to credible. Generic illustrations would not have.
  • Name the fear directly. The gift card mechanic only resolved when the flow explicitly told users that they buy the card and they use it themselves. Every indirect framing failed in Round 1.
  • Copy is the highest-leverage lever inside the flow. Most of the Round 1 friction concentrated in copy and language, not interaction design or layout. Word changes did more than redesigns.
  • A validation round changes the story, not just the data. Round 2 let the case study end on "this worked" instead of "we recommend." That distinction matters for what designers can say about their own work.
